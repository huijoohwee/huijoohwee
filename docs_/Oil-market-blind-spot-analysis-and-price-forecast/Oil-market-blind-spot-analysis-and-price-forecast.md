**The shared blind spot: symmetric recovery fallacy + resolution anchoring**

Every major institution — Goldman, UBS, Citi, HSBC, IEA — shares the same structural flaw wired into their models. Goldman pushed Q4 2026 Brent to $71 and WTI to $67, while UBS raised its full-year 2026 Brent average to $72. Those numbers look superficially different, but they are built on the same hidden assumption. Goldman projected that exports through the Strait of Hormuz will normalise by the end of June, and the bank modeled a slower recovery in Gulf oil production alongside that timeline. The IEA similarly presented a forecast assuming a resumption of regular deliveries from the Middle East to international markets by mid-year, though not back to pre-conflict levels. [TheStreet + 2](https://www.thestreet.com/investing/goldman-sachs-resets-oil-price-target-for-rest-of-2026)

Neither institution has a geopolitical model for *why* resolution happens on that timeline. It is mean-reversion anchoring — models calibrated to revert to long-run price equilibria, treating disruption resolution as automatic.

**Three compounding structural errors:**

**1. Symmetric recovery illusion.** Even following a hypothetical ceasefire, analysts estimate a minimum of two months before steady export flows could resume, with full infrastructure recovery potentially requiring six months or beyond. Tanker repositioning and war-risk insurance normalisation add weeks to effective recovery timelines even after physical routes reopen. Refined petroleum products and LNG have no equivalent pipeline bypass option — these flows are entirely dependent on maritime routing. Yet every institutional model shows price declining at roughly the same velocity it rose. [Discovery Alert](https://discoveryalert.com.au/strait-hormuz-oil-supply-shock-2026-brent-crude-prices/)[Discovery Alert](https://discoveryalert.com.au/strait-hormuz-oil-supply-disruption-price-impact-2026/)

**2. OPEC+ spare capacity stranded behind the chokepoint.** Combined spare bypass capacity across pipeline systems is estimated at approximately 2.6 mb/d — a meaningful volume, but one that represents only a fraction of typical Hormuz throughput. The Saudi, UAE, Kuwaiti, and Iraqi spare capacity that institutions cite as a "buffer" overwhelmingly transits the Strait itself. The OPEC+ quota increase represents less than two percent of the supply disrupted by the closure of the strait. [Discovery Alert](https://discoveryalert.com.au/strait-hormuz-oil-supply-disruption-price-impact-2026/)[Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/4/5/opec-agrees-to-hike-oil-output-warns-of-slow-recovery-after-attacks)

**3. Non-linear demand destruction ignored.** The EIA's initial assessment was that months of global oversupply and significant inventory builds had the market well-positioned to weather a short-term disruption. But as the conflict persisted, oil inventories continued to fall. Demand responds much more quickly to high prices than supply can respond. At $100–140/bbl, emerging market demand elasticity is sharply non-linear compared to the 2010s data used to calibrate institutional models. [U.S. Energy Information Administration](https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/steo/pdf/steo_full.pdf)

**The re-simulated trajectories**

Correcting for these three errors produces materially different paths than the consensus:

The **base scenario** (Hormuz partially reopening in August, asymmetric recovery through Q4) keeps Brent elevated — peaking near $120 in July, easing to ~$77 by November. That is $7–9 above the Goldman consensus at every quarter-end.

The **prolonged closure scenario** (conflict extending into Q4) reaches $140 in August — consistent with Barclays' warning that Brent could test $120 if a fleshed-out conflict persists, and JPMorgan's view that prices could spike above $150 if flows through the Strait remain disrupted into May. Demand destruction then carves $30 off the peak by November, leaving prices structurally elevated near $108. [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/4/5/opec-agrees-to-hike-oil-output-warns-of-slow-recovery-after-attacks)

The **swift resolution + demand collapse scenario** — the scenario closest to Goldman's assumption — is the least likely because it requires both geopolitical resolution *and* a clean demand snap-back. Instead, global oil demand growth averaged only 0.2 mb/d in 2026 under the May EIA forecast, with reductions concentrated in Asia, which is most reliant on Middle East crude. A swift reopening meeting already-suppressed demand would overshoot on the downside, reaching the high $60s by year-end. [U.S. Energy Information Administration](https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/steo/report/global_oil.php)

The deepest flaw is not being wrong about prices — it's that consensus models all converge on the same optimistic resolution calendar, leaving no institutional framework to price the asymmetric recovery that physical reality demands.
