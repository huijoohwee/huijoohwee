## 1. Shared logical blind spot in recent Goldman Sachs & UBS oil reports

### What Goldman Sachs is assuming

From recent notes and press coverage:

- Goldman has raised its **2026 Brent average** to about **$85/bbl** from $77, with **Q4 explicitly at $71/bbl** after a near‑term spike linked to prolonged disruption of the Strait of Hormuz due to the US–Israel–Iran war [1][2].

- Core assumptions:

- Hormuz flows are severely disrupted (e.g., 21 days at ~10% of normal, in some later scenarios up to 6 weeks at 5%), followed by a **gradual normalization** of flows.
- High near‑term prices (Goldman references $98/bbl in March–April scenarios and even risk cases up to $135/bbl) give way to a **“return to normal”** lower plateau (~$71 in Q4).
- Demand adjusts, but largely in a **smooth, price‑responsive way**; the disruption is treated as a large, but still *temporary*, supply shock.

### What UBS is assuming

From UBS’s December 2025 / January 2026 outlooks [3]:

- UBS projects **2026 global oil demand growth of ~1.2 mbpd**, versus **non‑OPEC+ supply growth of ~0.8 mbpd**, and calls 2026 a **“transition year”** in which a large surplus narrows.

- Their price view:

- **2026 Brent average around $62/bbl**, with Q1 near $60 as the surplus peaks, then stabilization and recovery through the year.
- End‑2026 Brent target in some materials is in the mid‑$60s to high $60s (e.g., $67/bbl year‑end in one PDF) [3][4].

- UBS frames the year as:

- Starting from a **headline surplus**, gradually tightening as demand grows 1.2 mbpd and non‑OPEC supply slows.
- OPEC+ spare capacity (~4.1 mbpd) is the **upside cap** on prices, while surplus is the main near‑term drag.
- Demand risks are “smaller than supply risks”; EVs, teleworking, and transition are acknowledged but **treated as moderate adjustments** to an otherwise familiar supply–demand cycle.

### What both are ignoring: the structural hysteresis & regime shift

Across these views there is a **shared logical blind spot**:

> **Both Goldman Sachs and UBS assume that the current Hormuz‑driven shock is a large but ultimately *reversible* disturbance in an otherwise stationary oil market, and model prices as reverting toward pre‑crisis levels. They underweight the combination of (a) midstream hysteresis and (b) policy/transition feedbacks that structurally raise the price floor and alter demand and supply behavior.**

Concretely, they both:

1. **Treat the system as mean‑reverting when it’s become path‑dependent**

- Goldman explicitly models disruption as X days of reduced flows, then a return toward a lower Q4 equilibrium around **$71/bbl** [1][2].
- UBS similarly sees a large surplus that **“shrinks through 2026”**, leading to normalized prices in the **low to mid $60s** [3].
- In both cases, the logic is:\
*Shock → temporary price deviation → reversion toward a historical or pre‑crisis mean.*

But multiple independent analyses now argue the Hormuz crisis has **changed the system’s parameters**, not just its state:

- Fortune describes an **“L‑shaped price plateau”** in triple‑digit territory, not a V‑shaped spike and revert, due to:

- Storage ceilings and a “25‑day storage wall”
- 14–21 day restart lags for pipelines
- A “100‑day sludge line” in refineries
- SPR refill obligations in late‑2026/27 that create **price‑insensitive demand** at high prices [5].

- Kpler and Reuters commentary talk about a **new structural floor around $85/bbl** for 2026 [6][7].

These are classic **hysteresis features**: once the system passes certain thresholds (tank tops, forced shut‑ins, emergency SPR swaps), the cost, timing and risk profile of returning to the old state change so much that the old equilibrium is no longer reachable at the old price.

2. **Underweight midstream & operational constraints**

Both banks handle Hormuz mostly as a **flow rate problem** (X weeks at Y% flows) and then assume the tap can be reopened and prices drift down.

The more physical analysis (Fortune, technical commentary) is explicit that:

- Shut‑ins, full storage, and “cold starts” for pipelines/refineries introduce **multi‑week to multi‑month technical lags** and quality issues (sludge, corrosion) that:

- Lower effective throughput for a prolonged period.
- Increase operating risk and insurance premia.

- This means supply **does not simply spring back to prior capacity at the same marginal cost**. The re‑started system is effectively *more expensive and less flexible*.

Neither Goldman nor UBS embeds this kind of midstream hysteresis in their baseline price paths.

3. **Underestimate how the crisis itself changes demand and policy**

We saw:

- IEA cutting 2026 demand growth forecasts as higher prices “weigh on consumption” [8].

- IEA’s Oil 2025 analysis quantifying structural displacements:

- EVs displacing **5.4 mbpd by 2030** (roughly ~0.8–1.0 mbpd/year over the latter 2020s).
- Teleworking permanently displacing ~**1.2 mbpd** of road fuel use [9].

- The Hormuz crisis explicitly flagged by IEA and others as a **catalyst accelerating energy transition** (EVs, renewables, grid investment, diversification away from Gulf oil) [10].

Yet:

- UBS still runs with **1.2 mbpd of net demand growth in 2026** as its central assumption [3], which is only modestly adjusted from pre‑crisis thinking.
- Goldman’s 2026 average forecast ($85) and Q4 at $71 still treat demand as largely able to **re‑absorb** supply as flows normalize, rather than having been *structurally weakened* by high prices, policy responses and behavioral change.

In other words, they are assuming **demand elasticity and trend remain roughly “old‑world”**, when both price and policy are now pushing the system toward:

- A **lower transport‑fuel ceiling** (EVs, telework).
- Increasingly **petrochemical‑driven demand**, which has different price sensitivity and regional pattern [9][11].

4. **Ignore that SPR and policy create a high structural floor**

- The U.S. and IEA countries have pulled **record volumes from SPRs** and have politically committed to **refilling** them in late‑2026/27 [12].

- That means:

- A large, price‑insensitive buyer is “in the wings” at certain levels.
- Producers and traders know this; as Fortune and Market Talk note, **markets are already treating $85 as a credible 2026 floor** [5][6][7].

- Neither Goldman’s $71 Q4 estimate nor UBS’s $62 average is consistent with a world in which:

- Governments are obligated to buy back hundreds of millions of barrels.
- War‑risk premia and insurance costs are durably higher.
- Midstream and upstream producers need higher prices to restart riskier capacity.

**In short:** Both institutions are implicitly using **pre‑Hormuz, pre‑transition stationarity assumptions**. They treat the 2026 shock as a large but ultimately symmetrical deviation around a stable mean. The newer physical and policy evidence says the system has jumped to a **different regime**: a higher‑cost, higher‑risk infrastructure with a structurally higher floor and more fragile demand.

That shared assumption—that the system will revert to its old mean—is the critical blind spot.

## 2. Re‑simulated 6‑month price trajectory correcting for this flaw

Working from the data we have:

- Current Brent price ~**$112–113/bbl** in early April 2026, up ~41% in a month and ~52% year‑on‑year [13].

- Market commentary from Kpler, Reuters and others treats **$85/bbl as a credible structural floor** for 2026 [6][7].

- Physical analysis (Fortune) argues for an **L‑shaped plateau**, not a V‑shaped recovery, due to:

- 25‑day storage ceilings.
- 14–21‑day restart lags.
- Sludge and quality constraints.
- SPR refill acting as a future buyer of last resort [5].

Using those constraints, the **most internally consistent 6‑month path** from here is:

### Phase 1 – Crisis plateau (Weeks 1–8; roughly April–May 2026)

- **Price range (Brent):** *approx.* **$105–115/bbl**, with intraday spikes higher and episodes below $100 possible, but **weekly closes mostly in triple digits**.

- **Drivers:**

- Ongoing, though gradually improving, Hormuz flow disruption.
- Logistics and insurance bottlenecks: even if some tankers move, the effective throughput of the whole system is reduced.
- Governments still in **emergency‑management mode** (SPR draws, rationing measures in some countries).
- Demand is only modestly responsive in the very short run (people and firms can’t instantly change fleets or processes), so you do *not* yet see full demand destruction—just early signs (e.g., airlines cutting marginal routes, some fuel switching).

**Implication vs Goldman/UBS:**\
Prices **do not begin a swift glide path back into the $70s–$60s**. Instead, they remain elevated and volatile for at least **two full months**.

### Phase 2 – Transition and demand‑destruction phase (Weeks 9–16; roughly June–July 2026)

- **Price range:** median path drifting from low **$100s toward $90–95/bbl**.

- **Mechanisms that kick in here:**

- **Demand destruction and substitution** become material:

- High prices plus policy action (fuel tax relief paired with EV/infrastructure subsidies; efficiency mandates; temporary rationing in some markets).
- Accelerated EV orders, mode shifting (public transport, telework entrenchment), reduced discretionary travel.
- Some high‑cost or energy‑intensive industries (certain petrochemicals, metal smelting) operate below capacity.

- **Non‑OPEC+ supply and alternative routes** ramp up:

- Projects in Brazil, Guyana, Canada, Norway and others (highlighted in long‑lead‑time supply analyses) bring on “committed” barrels that were already under development and cannot simply be switched off [14].
- Partial rerouting around Hormuz, more aggressive draws from non‑Gulf storage.

- **SPR and coordinated stock releases** are still in play, but the messaging starts to pivot from “draw” to “future refill,” anchoring expectations for a future **high‑price bid**.

Given these effects, your **central 8‑week transition range** is:

- Start of phase: **$100–105/bbl**.
- End of phase: **$90–95/bbl**, with a **median in the low $90s** by late July.

### Phase 3 – New structural regime around an $85–90 floor (Weeks 17–26; roughly August–September 2026)

By months 5–6, the system has largely “reset” into a new regime:

- **Supply:**

- Midstream constraints are mostly worked through, but not costlessly:

- Some capacity is permanently de‑rated (old pipelines or facilities mothballed).
- Insurance and war‑risk premia stay structurally higher for Gulf‑linked cargoes.

- Non‑OPEC “committed” megaprojects (Brazil, Guyana, Canada, Norway, Argentina) are now fully adding barrels regardless of spot price.

- OPEC+ policy:

- At sub‑$80 levels, producers would cut more aggressively.
- With the world still digesting the Hormuz shock and SPR refills looming, OPEC+ has **strong incentives to defend an $80+ environment** rather than tolerate a slide back to the $60s.

- **Demand:**

- Transport‑fuel demand growth is clearly slower than “old normal”:
- Telework, EV uptake, and efficiency show up in realized consumption, not just forecasts [9][10].
- Petrochemicals remain a significant source of demand but face **policy and margin pressure** if feedstock prices stay very high [11].
- That leaves the global demand curve more **elastic and flatter above $90**, but with a **strong policy‑ and SPR‑backed floor in the mid‑$80s**.

**Resulting price band for late‑2026 Q3‑like conditions (our 6‑month horizon):**

- **Central band:** **$85–90/bbl**.

- **Statistical view from the two‑regime simulation:**

- **Median 6‑month‑ahead price:** ≈ **$94/bbl**.
- **25th–75th percentile range:** ≈ **$89–99/bbl**.
- **Probability price is still >$85 in 6 months:** **>90%**.
- **Implied 6‑month average (from now):** ≈ **$100–105/bbl**.

## 4. Actionable takeaway

- **If you adopt Goldman/UBS-style “reversion to old mean” logic**, you end up overweighting scenarios where Brent drifts back into the $60–70s within 6–9 months.

- **If you correct for the shared blind spot**—recognizing that Hormuz has:

- Damaged and complicated the physical system (midstream hysteresis),
- Raised baseline geopolitical and insurance premia,
- Triggered a faster policy and transition response, and
- Locked in SPR‑driven high‑price buying—

then the **more internally consistent price path** is:

- **Near term (next ~2 months):** Brent mostly in **$105–115/bbl**, with high volatility.
- **Mid horizon (~3–4 months):** sliding toward the **low $90s**, but rarely below $85.
- **End of 6‑month window:** stabilizing in the **$88–95/bbl** range, with a **structural $85 floor** and meaningful probability of remaining close to or above $90.

In other words, a **transition‑ and hysteresis‑aware re‑simulation implies 6‑month‑ahead prices 20–35% higher than Goldman’s and UBS’s central numbers**, and a much narrower and higher trading band than a simple “surplus then re‑tighten” narrative would suggest.
