- Goldman: Focuses on supply shock, assumes demand will recover and stay strong
- UBS: Focuses on supply-demand balance, assumes linear demand growth The logical flaw: **Both treat demand as a single, monolithic variable when it's actually bifurcating into two distinct markets: traditional fuel demand (peaking) and petrochemical feedstock demand (growing).** This is a key insight from the IEA report I extracted earlier: "Petrochemical feedstocks increasingly drive oil demand, with demand growth from petrochemicals outweighing transport-fuel growth from 2026 onward." So they both miss that:
1. Demand is not homogeneous
2. The growth is increasingly from petrochemicals, which has different price elasticity
3. The transport fuel segment is peaking, which is the bulk of price-sensitive demand This is a logical blind spot: treating a heterogeneous demand as homogeneous. Now, how does this affect price? If transport fuel demand is price-sensitive and peaking, while petrochemical demand is less sensitive, then:
- High prices will disproportionately affect transport fuel
- The overall demand curve becomes more kinked
- Prices may stay higher than expected because petrochemical demand provides a floor

## Shared Logical Blind Spot: Underestimation of Structural Hysteresis and Regime Shift

4. **Path Dependence**: The disruption accelerates energy transition (EVs, efficiency) and alters OPEC+ behavior in ways that change the long-term equilibrium

This is a logical fallacy because it violates the **non-stationarity principle**—you cannot apply historical mean-reversion logic to a system whose underlying parameters have been permanently altered by the shock itself.
